    Tips for the next six weeks

    Read the first one today. Come back to the rest whenever you need them.

    The rule that matters most
    You only get to sit the qualifier if your weekly assignments are good enough. IITM takes the best 2 of the first 3 assignment scores in each subject, and you need an average of at least 40% in every one of the four subjects.
    • Treat weeks 1, 2 and 3 assignments like mini exams. A weak score in one subject can block the whole exam even if the other three are great.
    • Submit a day before the deadline, never on the last night. Portals slow down when everyone rushes.
    • Check the real deadlines on the portal in week 1 and move the Thursday assignment days in this plan if they differ.
    • Do the week 4 assignment too. It doesn't count for eligibility, but it is the best practice you'll get.
    Computational Thinking
    • Don't just watch the lectures. Pause before each answer is revealed and predict it yourself.
    • Trace every pseudocode by hand with a small table: one column per variable, one row per step. This single habit fixes most CT mistakes.
    • The usual traps are forgetting to initialise a variable, updating it in the wrong place, and miscounting how many times a nested loop runs.
    • Redo every example from the lectures on paper without looking. If you can't, that's tomorrow's first 15 minutes.
    • For the dataset cards (scores, words, shopping bills), practise asking your own questions: how many cards pass this filter, what's the max, what's the sum.
    • Give CT your freshest hour of the day. It needs your sharpest brain.
    Maths 1
    • Build a one page formula sheet as you go, not at the end. Add to it every day.
    • Sketch a quick graph whenever there's a function, line or parabola. Pictures make the algebra obvious.
    • Solve problems more than you rewatch videos. Ten solved questions beat one extra lecture.
    • For sets and relations, test each property with a tiny example before trusting your answer.
    Stats 1
    • Always ask first: is this data categorical or numerical? Half the questions depend on that answer.
    • Remember the four scales in order: nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio. Each one allows everything the previous one does, plus a little more.
    • Calculate a mean, median and standard deviation fully by hand at least once, so the formulas stop feeling abstract.
    • You already love data. Stats is the subject where that shows. Enjoy it.
    English 1
    • This is the most scoreable subject. Don't skip it just because it feels easy.
    • Say the sounds and stressed syllables out loud. Reading them silently doesn't stick.
    • Keep a short list of grammar rules you got wrong and read it before every assignment.
    Building your own timetable
    • Put CT at the time of day you feel sharpest, and English when you're most tired. It's the lightest.
    • Leave gaps. A timetable with no breaks is a timetable you'll abandon by noon.
    • Plan tonight for tomorrow. Waking up to a ready list removes the first decision of the day.
    • If yesterday worked, copy it. Consistency beats reinventing the day.
    Staying focused
    • Use the Focus tab. A visible timer makes it easier to ignore everything else.
    • When you can't start, commit to just two minutes. Starting is the hardest part; continuing is easy.
    • Move during breaks: a walk, stretches, stairs. Sitting still for hours drains focus.
    • Sleep is part of studying. Memory is built overnight, so a late night costs more than it gives.
    • Tick things off the moment you finish them. That little moment of progress is real fuel.
    On a bad day
    • Do the minimum day: 30 minutes of CT and one assignment question. That's still a win.
    • Don't try to catch up everything tomorrow. Just carry on with tomorrow's plan and fold the missed topic into Sunday.
    • Missing one day is normal. Missing two in a row is when to pause and ask what's going on.
    • Be as kind to yourself as you are to everyone else. You deserve it too.
    Mocks and revision
    • Take mocks exactly like the real thing: timed, phone away, no pausing.
    • Spend as long analysing a mock as feels reasonable, then a bit more. The learning is in the review.
    • Tag each mistake as concept, careless, or time. Each one needs a different fix.
    • In the last two days, no new topics. Only your formula sheet, one page summaries and your mistake notebook.
    Exam day
    • Pack the night before: hall ticket, photo ID and anything else the hall ticket asks for. Follow its instructions over anything written here.
    • Check the route to the centre in advance and leave early.
    • Eat something light and familiar.
    • If a question stumps you, mark it and move on. Come back with fresh eyes.
    • You did the work. Walk in knowing that.
